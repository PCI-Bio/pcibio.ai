AI-assisted biomarker assessment.

How machine learning turns manual slide scoring into consistent, quantitative measurement, and what that means for reproducibility across laboratories and clinical trials.

Learning objectives

  • Recognise how an algorithm produces a quantitative score from a whole-slide image
  • Understand why standardization across sites matters for clinical-trial data

Section 01

The problem AI is solving

Manual IHC interpretation, a pathologist looking at a stained slide and estimating what share of cells stain positive and how strongly, has been the standard for decades. It works, but it has a known limitation: it is inherently subjective.

That subjectivity has clinical consequences. Inter-observer variability is a documented feature of manual IHC, not an edge case. For biomarkers where a scoring threshold decides treatment eligibility (HER2 for targeted therapy, MMR for immunotherapy, PD-L1 for checkpoint blockade) the inconsistency affects which patients receive which treatment. The same pathologist can also score the same slide differently on two days.

Scale adds a third constraint. A single whole-slide image can contain millions of cells. Manual scoring estimates from a representative sample rather than every cell, a practical necessity that adds sampling variability on top of observer variability. The more heterogeneous the tumor, the more that matters.

Section 02

What AI-assisted assessment does

Machine learning lets an algorithm learn patterns from annotated data and apply them to new images. In digital pathology that enables automated image analysis: the algorithm scans the whole digitised slide, finds individual cells, classifies each by staining intensity, and produces a quantitative score over the full section rather than a sample, in a fraction of the time.

A published comparison between manual analysis and one such tool, IHC Profiler, reported an 88.6 % match rate, which shows that automated scoring can align closely with expert interpretation while being far more consistent across repeated runs and laboratories.

Manual scoring

Estimate · observer-dependent

  • 1The pathologist scans a representative sample of cells
  • 2Estimates staining intensity and the share of positive cells
  • 3Records a score from trained visual judgement

AI-assisted scoring

Quantitative · consistent

  • 1The algorithm scans the whole slide at cell level
  • 2Classifies every cell by staining intensity
  • 3Outputs a quantitative score from the full section
Assistant, not replacement. Regulatory-approved AI diagnostic tools are designated assistive: they provide a score as input to the pathologist's review, not an independent diagnosis. The pathologist reviews the output, applies clinical context and makes the final call. That is a deliberate design choice, not a temporary limitation. The PCI-AI reading room is built the same way: the pathologist reviews, edits the summary and signs.
Section 03

Why standardization matters

One of the biggest advantages of algorithmic scoring is reproducibility across sites. A manual score from one laboratory may not be comparable with one from another, even with the same antibody and protocol, because pathologists calibrate their visual estimates differently. A validated model applies the same criteria regardless of who runs it or where.

That matters most in clinical trials, where scores from patients at different institutions must be directly comparable for the data to be interpretable. It also matters for emerging biomarkers whose thresholds are still being set: consistent measurement is what makes the evidence reliable.

Consistent across observersThe same criteria applied to every slide, every time, removing reader-to-reader variation in how intensity is judged.
Full tissue coverageEvery cell in the section is scored rather than a sample, which reduces sampling bias in heterogeneous tumors.
Reproducible across sitesA validated model gives the same output at any participating laboratory, so cross-site data compare directly.
Section 04

Where this is headed

The next generation of biomarkers may be defined by AI-first assays, tests designed without a manual-scoring equivalent. Rather than automating an existing score, such systems would find patterns that visual inspection cannot: spatial relationships between cell types, subtle texture, microenvironment signatures, and links between morphology and treatment response or survival.

Those capabilities rest on the same infrastructure as today's assisted scoring: whole-slide imaging, standardized tissue preparation and validated antibody staining. The direction is more computation in the standard pathology workflow, not as a replacement for expertise but as a tool that widens what can be measured.

Reliable AI starts with a reliable stain. A model trained to score a marker at a given intensity can only perform consistently if the stain itself is consistent run to run.
