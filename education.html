Foundations 01

What is IHC?

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) is a laboratory technique that uses antibodies to detect specific proteins in tissue samples. It is one of the most widely used diagnostic tools in surgical pathology: it lets a pathologist see which proteins a tissue is expressing, and at what level, directly on a stained glass slide.

The technique applies a targeted antibody to a thin slice of tissue mounted on a glass slide. If the protein that antibody is designed to detect is present, the antibody binds to it. A detection system then turns that binding into a visible colour, most often the brown stain DAB, so the pathologist can see exactly where, and how strongly, the protein is expressed.

Many cancers are defined not just by how cells look under a microscope but by which proteins those cells express. IHC answers questions such as: is this tumor expressing a receptor a targeted therapy could act on? Has it lost a protein that normally suppresses growth? Is this protein present at a normal level, an elevated level, or not at all? Those answers shape treatment decisions, immunotherapy eligibility and how a cancer is formally classified.

A standard IHC slide has two colour components. Hematoxylin (blue to purple) is a counterstain that colours every nucleus and gives a reference map of the tissue. DAB (brown) is the specific signal that appears only where the target protein is present. A pathologist reading the slide looks at where the brown signal appears, how intense it is, and what share of cells show it. No brown means the protein is absent or below the detection threshold; strong, widespread brown means high expression.

Foundations 02

What is H&E staining?

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining is the most fundamental technique in histopathology. It is the first stain applied to virtually every tissue sample submitted for diagnosis, and it remains the primary way pathologists assess tissue architecture and cell morphology.

H&E uses two dyes in sequence. Hematoxylin binds to nucleic acids in the nucleus and stains it blue to purple. Because every cell has a nucleus, hematoxylin gives a complete picture of cell distribution and density across the tissue. Eosin binds to proteins in the cytoplasm and the extracellular matrix and stains them pink, revealing the cell body and the surrounding connective tissue.

Together the two stains show how cells are organised, how densely they are packed, and whether that organisation looks normal or abnormal. On a well-prepared H&E slide a pathologist can judge the size, shape and appearance of individual cells. Enlarged nuclei, irregular nuclear borders and a high nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio are all associated with malignancy. The slide also shows tumor boundaries and the stroma around the tumor: connective tissue, vessels and immune infiltrates.

H&E reveals structure, but it cannot say which proteins a cell is expressing. Two tumors that look alike on H&E can have completely different molecular profiles and need different treatment. That is why IHC is used alongside it: H&E establishes what the tissue looks like, IHC establishes what proteins it expresses. Foundations 03

What is a biomarker?

A biomarker is a measurable biological characteristic that tells you something about a normal or abnormal process. In pathology, biomarkers are usually proteins, or the genes that encode them, whose presence, absence or level in tumor tissue carries clinical meaning.

Biomarkers classify tumors when morphology alone is ambiguous, predict prognosis where certain profiles go with more or less aggressive disease, and guide treatment where a targeted therapy or immunotherapy only works in tumors that express a specific marker.

IHC is one of the primary ways to measure a biomarker in tissue. The pathologist applies an antibody against the marker and evaluates the intensity of the staining, the percentage of cells that stain, and where in the cell the signal sits: nucleus, cytoplasm or membrane. Different biomarkers use different scoring systems. Some are reported as positive or negative. Others use a continuous scale such as the H-score. The system depends on the marker and on the clinical question.

Four examples. p53 is a tumor suppressor encoded by the TP53 gene; its IHC pattern (null, wild-type or overexpression) reflects the underlying mutation status and is used in cancer classification. MLH1, MSH2, MSH6 and PMS2 are mismatch repair proteins; loss of nuclear expression of one or more indicates deficient mismatch repair, which bears on immunotherapy eligibility. PD-L1 expression in tumor and immune cells is used to predict response to checkpoint inhibitors. HER2, when overexpressed, makes certain breast and gastric cancers eligible for HER2-targeted therapy.

Results from one laboratory need to be comparable with another, above all in clinical trials that enrol patients at many sites. Inconsistent antibody concentrations, protocols or scoring criteria can produce different results from the same tissue and change which treatment a patient receives. Standardized controls, validated antibodies and objective scoring tools are what make a biomarker result reproducible.

The study behind the controls

A cell line–based immunohistochemical p53 expression pattern control panel

Three ovarian cancer cell lines, one for each TP53 status (overexpression, wild-type and null), were assembled into a single p53 control panel. Pathologists using the panel as a reference agreed with each other more than with standard tissue controls alone, and read null expression as wild-type less often, the most consequential p53 error in gynecologic pathology. Next-generation sequencing of the cell lines and of the discordant cases gave a molecular basis for the disagreements. This panel is the foundation of PCI's TriControl product.
